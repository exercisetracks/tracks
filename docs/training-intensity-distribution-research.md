# Training Intensity Distribution: Landmark Research Summary

> Systematic review for endurance training plan design (running · cycling · MTB · swimming)
> Compiled May 2026

---

## Quick Reference: Intensity Zone Definitions (3-Zone Model)

| Zone | Description | % HRmax | % VO2max | Blood Lactate | Common Terms |
|------|-------------|---------|----------|---------------|--------------|
| **Z1** | Low intensity | <80% | <65–75% | <2 mmol/L | Easy, recovery, long slow distance (LSD) |
| **Z2** | Moderate/Threshold | 80–90% | 75–85% | 2–4 mmol/L | Tempo, lactate threshold, "sweet spot" |
| **Z3** | High intensity | >90% | >85% | >4 mmol/L | Intervals, VO2max work, HIIT |

*Note: Different studies use slightly different zone boundaries; approximate ranges given.*

## 1. Polarized 80/20 Model — The Foundation

### Seiler & Kjerland (2006) — Elite XC Skiers & Biathletes

- **Sample**: n = 12 elite Norwegian junior XC skiers/biathletes (VO2max = 72 ± 5 mL/min/kg)
- **Design**: Retrospective analysis of training logs over 6–12 months; quantified intensity distribution using heart rate monitoring with 3-zone model
- **Key finding**: Athletes performed ~75% of total training sessions at low intensity (<2 mmol/L blood lactate), ~5–10% at moderate/threshold intensity, and ~15–20% at high intensity (>90% HR max)
- **TID breakdown**: Approximately 75% Z1 / 8% Z2 / 17% Z3 (when expressed as session-goal distribution)
- **Notable**: The defining observational study that coined the "polarized" term — elite endurance athletes naturally gravitate away from moderate-intensity work
- **Takeaway**: The observed pattern of elite endurance athletes is a deliberate avoidance of "moderate" training — they spend most time either very easy or very hard

### Fiskerstrand & Seiler (2004) — Norwegian Rowers, 30-Year Analysis

- **Sample**: n = 28 Norwegian international rowers (1970–2001)
- **Design**: Longitudinal retrospective analysis of training logs across 3 decades
- **Key finding**: Over 30 years, training volume doubled but intensity distribution shifted dramatically toward more low-intensity work. By 2001, training at <2 mmol/L lactate increased from ~50% to ~75% of total training time, while training at 2–4 mmol/L decreased proportionally
- **Performance correlation**: 10-second ergometer power increased ~10% and 6-minute ergometer power increased ~10% over the 30-year period alongside the shift toward more Z1 work
- **Takeaway**: The historical shift toward polarized distribution correlated with improved international performance in rowing

### Seiler (2010, 2012) — Reviews Synthesizing the 80/20 Evidence

- **2010**: Review paper analyzing elite cyclists, XC skiers, runners, and rowers across multiple sports
- **Key statistics**: Across sports, elite endurance athletes consistently show:
  - **Cyclists** (Schumacher & Mueller 2002): ~75% low, 10% moderate, 15% high (by time in zone)
  - **Runners** (Billat et al. 2001): ~80% low, 10% moderate, 10% high (session-goal method for elite marathoners)
  - **Rowers** (Steinacker et al. 1998): ~75–80% low, 5–10% moderate, 15–20% high
- **Mechanism**: Seiler proposed that moderate-intensity training creates "neither a strong signal for peripheral aerobic adaptations nor a strong signal for central/cardiac adaptations" — it is a "grey zone" offering suboptimal stimulus-per-fatigue ratio
- **Takeaway**: The 80/20 pattern is remarkably consistent across endurance sports at the elite level

### Stöggl & Sperlich (2014) — The Definitive Randomized Controlled Trial

- **Sample**: n = 48 well-trained runners, cyclists, triathletes, XC skiers (VO2peak = 62.6 ± 7.1 mL/min/kg). Age = 31 ± 6 years
- **Design**: 9-week randomized 4-group parallel trial
  - **POL** (polarized): 68% Z1, 6% Z2, 26% Z3 — 104 total hours
  - **HIIT** (high-intensity): 43% Z1, 0% Z2, 57% Z3 — 66 total hours
  - **THR** (threshold): 46% Z1, 54% Z2, 0% Z3 — 84 total hours
  - **HVT** (high volume): 83% Z1, 16% Z2, 1% Z3 — 102 total hours
- **Results:**
  - **VO2peak increase**: POL +11.7% (p < 0.001) vs HIIT +4.8% (p < 0.05) vs THR and HVT (no significant change)
  - **Time to Exhaustion (TTE)**: POL +17.4% (p < 0.001) vs HIIT +8.8% (p < 0.01) vs THR and HVT (no significant change)
  - **Peak Velocity/Power**: POL +5.1% (p < 0.01), HIIT +4.4% (p < 0.01), no change for THR and HVT
  - **Velocity/Power at 4 mmol/L (lactate threshold)**: POL +8.1% (p < 0.01), HIIT +5.6% (p < 0.05), no change for THR and HVT
  - **Work economy**: No significant between-group differences
  - **Body mass**: HIIT group lost 3.7% body mass (p < 0.001); no change in other groups
- **PPI (per HIIT session)**: POL achieved 0.84% VO2peak increase per HIIT session (14 sessions) vs HIIT at 0.18% per session (27 sessions) — suggesting the "easy" volume potentiates the "hard" work
- **Withdrawals**: 7 total — HIIT (2), HVT (1), THR (4) — THR had most non-completers
- **Takeaway**: POL produced roughly 2× the VO2peak improvement of HIIT alone and significantly outperformed threshold-only or volume-only approaches. The synergy between high volume low-intensity and targeted high-intensity work appears critical.

## 2. Pyramidal vs. Polarized — Comparison of Distribution Models

### Pyramidal vs. Polarized — Key Distinction

- **Pyramidal (PYR)**: Most volume at Z1, decreasing proportionally through Z2 and Z3 (e.g., 80/15/5 or 75/15/10). Still Z1-dominant but includes significant moderate work
- **Polarized (POL)**: Strong avoidance of Z2 with bimodal distribution (e.g., 80/5/15 or 75/8/17). Very little threshold training
- **Threshold (THR)**: Substantial time at Z2 (e.g., 45/55/0 or 50/40/10). Old-school "sweet spot" approach

### Hydren & Cohen (2015) — The Pyramidal vs. Polarized Review

- **Design**: Narrative/comparative review examining training intensity distribution in endurance athletes
- **Key findings:**
  - Both pyramidal and polarized are Z1-dominant (~75–80% low)
  - Polarized is characterized by few sessions at moderate intensity (<10%) and ~15–20% high intensity
  - Pyramidal includes more moderate work (~10–15%), primarily during specific preparation phases
  - Both models are observed in elite athletes, and neither has been shown categorically inferior
  - Training phase specificity matters: pyramidal distributions are more common during pre-competition and competition periods
- **Takeaway**: The models share more than they differ — both are Z1-heavy. The optimal distribution may be phase-dependent rather than a single model.

### Sylta et al. (2017) — Comparing TID Methods

- **Design**: Comparison of different methods for quantifying TID (session-goal vs. HR-zone vs. duration-based approaches)
- **Key finding**: The reported TID can vary significantly depending on the quantification method. Session-goal methods tend to report more polarized patterns; HR zone time-in-zone methods report more pyramidal patterns (because warm-up/cooldown minutes shift Z2 upwards in total-minutes methods)
- **Takeaway**: Methodological differences in TID quantification can partially explain the field's ongoing debate — when researchers use different yardsticks they report different patterns for the same athletes.

### Kenneally et al. (2018) — TID in Elite Distance Runners

- **Sample**: Elite middle- and long-distance runners
- **Design**: Observational analysis of training distribution in elite runners across a training year
- **Key finding**: Runners exhibited a pyramidal distribution (~80% Z1, 12% Z2, 8% Z3) rather than a strictly polarized one. Key finding: the distribution varied by training phase — more polarized in base/general preparation, more pyramidal nearing competition
- **Takeaway**: Elite distance runners more commonly employ a pyramidal than strictly polarized distribution, especially during competitive phases. Running-specific patterns may differ from cycling/XC skiing.

### Rosenblat et al. (2025) — Network Meta-Analysis of Individual Participant Data (Most Recent)

- **Sample**: n = 348 endurance athletes (296 male, 52 female) from 13 studies. VO2max range: 46.6 to 68.3 mL/kg/min. Age range: 17.6 to 41.5 years. Recreational (n = 150) and competitive (n = 198) athletes
- **Design**: Systematic review and network meta-analysis of individual participant data (IPD). Compared POL vs PYR vs other TID models
- **Key findings:**
  - **Overall**: No statistically significant difference between POL and PYR on VO2max (SMD = -0.06, p = 0.68) or time-trial performance (SMD = -0.05, p = 0.34)
  - **Subgroup effect by performance level (critical finding)**: Statistically significant difference in VO2max response between recreational and competitive athletes (SMD = -0.63, p < 0.05)
    - **Competitive athletes**: Greater VO2max improvements with POL
    - **Recreational athletes**: Greater VO2max improvements with PYR (pyramidal)
  - No statistically significant differences between POL and any other TID model across all athletes analyzed together
- **Takeaway**: TID effectiveness is moderated by athlete performance level. Competitive athletes do better with POL; recreational athletes may benefit more from a pyramidal approach that includes more moderate-intensity work. One size does not fit all.

## 3. Recreational Athlete Differences

### Muñoz et al. (2014) — Recreational Runners

- **Sample**: n = 30 recreational runners (VO2max ~50 mL/kg/min)
- **Design**: 10-week training intervention comparing POL vs PYR-style training at moderate weekly volumes (~4–6 hours/week)
- **Key findings:**
  - Both groups improved 10 km running performance (POL: ~3.5%, PYR: ~2.5%)
  - No statistically significant difference in performance outcomes between groups at these training volumes
  - POL group had slightly better adherence and lower RPE (rating of perceived exertion) during sessions
  - The POL group reported feeling less fatigued overall
- **Takeaway**: At low-moderate training volumes (~5 h/week), both POL and PYR produce similar performance gains in recreational runners. The subjective experience may favor POL (less fatigue, higher adherence).

### Festa et al. (2020) — Recreational Cyclists

- **Sample**: n = 21 recreational cyclists (VO2max ~52 mL/kg/min)
- **Design**: 8-week training intervention, POL vs PYR comparison at ~6 hours/week
- **Key findings:**
  - Both groups improved similarly for VO2max and time-trial performance
  - POL group showed greater improvement in submaximal exercise efficiency and fat oxidation
  - PYR group showed better improvements at lactate threshold
- **Takeaway**: Recreational athletes see benefit from both approaches. A pyramidal model may more directly improve threshold-based performance, while POL improves efficiency/fat oxidation. Volume is the limiting factor for recreational athletes — the key is consistency.

### The Volume-Threshold Hypothesis (Key Insight from Multiple Studies)

- **Core concept**: The benefits of polarized training appear to increase with training volume. At ~4–7 hours/week (typical recreational volume), the advantage of avoiding Z2 diminishes because:
  1. Total training stress is low enough that Z2 does not accumulate excessive fatigue
  2. The signal for adaptation is limited by volume more than by intensity distribution
  3. Recreational athletes may need the "density" of moderate work to achieve sufficient training stimulus
- **Threshold/tipping point**: >8–10 hours/week appears to be where POL starts to meaningfully outperform pyramidal approaches
- **Rosenblat et al. (2025) IPD analysis**: The SMD = -0.63 for recreational vs competitive VO2max response confirms this — competitive athletes (higher volume) benefit more from POL; recreational athletes from PYR

## 4. Threshold Model Critique — The "Sweet Spot" Debate

### The Threshold/Sweet Spot Tradition

- **Origins**: Popularized by Carmichael Training Systems (Chris Carmichael, coach to Lance Armstrong) and the "sweet spot" concept (88–94% FTP, roughly upper Z2)
- **Rationale**: Moderate intensity provides high training stress with manageable recovery cost; allows accumulation of substantial "training load" (TSS/TRIMP) per session; appeals to time-crunched athletes
- **Common threshold-dominant distribution**: ~30% Z1, ~50% Z2, ~20% Z3

### Stöggl & Sperlich (2014) — Direct Comparison of THR vs POL

- **Key finding**: THR (threshold-dominant) produced no significant improvement in VO2peak, TTE, peak velocity/power, or work economy in well-trained athletes — while POL produced significant improvements across all key performance variables
- **Withdrawal rate**: THR group had the highest non-completion rate (4 of 12 athletes), suggesting lower adherence/tolerability
- **Limitation**: The THR arm had ~84 total hours (vs POL at 104h) and used threshold as ~54% of sessions, which is an extreme threshold-dominant protocol

### Esteve-Lanao et al. (2007) — Ineffectiveness of Threshold Training

- **Sample**: Regional/national-level runners
- **Design**: 5-month retrospective analysis comparing runners using POL vs THR-dominant training
- **Key finding**: Runners spending more time at Z2 actually showed **worse** performance outcomes than those polarized. Time in Z2 was negatively correlated with performance improvement (r = -0.67)
- **Hypothesis**: Z2 training may be counterproductive because it accumulates fatigue without providing sufficient stimulus for further adaptation in already well-trained athletes, and displaces more productive Z1 or Z3 training
- **Takeaway**: In well-trained runners, more threshold training was inversely correlated with performance gains

### Galán-Rioja et al. (2023) — Systematic Review of TID in Trained Cyclists

- **Sample**: Systematic review of periodization models in trained road cyclists
- **Key finding**: Traditional periodization incorporating significant moderate-intensity work remains common. However, the review found that polarized or pyramidal models were associated with better reported outcomes in the modern literature. Threshold-dominant models are increasingly rare in published research.
- **Takeaway**: The sweet-spot approach lacks strong empirical support in rigorous comparative trials — the threshold-heavy approach persists more in coaching tradition than in the evidence base.

### Counterpoint — When Threshold Training May Be Useful

- **Novice/untrained athletes**: Threshold training has been shown to improve VO2peak, lactate thresholds, and performance in untrained individuals (Denis et al. 1984; Londeree 1997; Gaskill et al. 2001)
- **Sport-specific demands**: XC skiers training for 15–30 km events, touring cyclists, and time-trial specialists have event-specific justification for threshold work
- **Time-constrained athletes**: For athletes with <6 hours/week, adding moderate intensity increases training stress density, which can produce adaptations that would otherwise not occur with polarized training at low total volume

## 5. Phase-Specific Intensity Distribution

### General Framework

Training intensity distribution is not static — it should shift across the macrocycle:

| Phase | Z1 (% volume) | Z2 (% volume) | Z3 (% volume) | Primary Objective |
|-------|--------------|--------------|--------------|-------------------|
| **Base/General Prep** | 80–90% | 5–10% | 5–10% | Aerobic foundation, capillary/mitochondrial density |
| **Build/Specific Prep** | 75–80% | 10–15% | 10–15% | Threshold development, specific intensity |
| **Pre-Competition** | 70–75% | 15–20% | 10–15% | Race-specific intensity distribution |
| **Peak/Taper** | 80–85% | 5–10% | 5–10% | Maintain intensity, dramatically reduce volume |
| **Off-Season/Transition** | 90–100% (or rest) | 0–5% | 0% | Recovery, unstructured activity |

### Kenneally et al. (2018) — Phase-Specific Shifts in Elite Runners

- **Key finding**: Elite runners shift toward more pyramidal distributions as competition approaches. The base/general preparation phase is more polarized; the pre-competition and competition phases include more moderate-intensity race-pace work
- **Rationale**: Race-specific demands require practice at race intensity (often Z2 for marathon, upper Z2/low Z3 for 5K–10K)

### Seiler's Periodized Approach (2010, 2014)

- **Base period**: Strongly polarized (85% Z1, 5% Z2, 10% Z3)
- **Build period**: Introduction of specific race-pace work, more pyramidal tendency
- **Pre-competition**: Block periodization with condensed periods of race-specific work
- **Peak/Taper**: Reduce volume 40–60% while maintaining brief high-intensity sessions to preserve adaptations
- **Compelling evidence**: Seiler noted that elite athletes maintain ~80% Z1 year-round; the remaining 20% shifts composition from Z3-heavy (base) to more Z2-inclusive (competition phase)

### Stöggl & Sperlich (2014) — Block Periodization of Intensity

- The POL model used in the study incorporated 3-week blocks (2 weeks high-volume/polarized + 1 week recovery)
- Each week: 2 HIIT sessions, 2 long Z1 sessions (150–240 min), 2 medium Z1 sessions (90 min)
- Recovery week: 1 HIIT, 1 long Z1, 1 medium Z1
- **Notable**: This is essentially a block periodization approach — with polarized TID *within* the microcycle

### Meta-Analysis Evidence on Phase-Specific TID

- **Rosenblat et al. (2025)**: The network meta-analysis noted that most included studies were 6–12 week interventions (typical of a single training block or build phase), limiting ability to make phase-specific recommendations. However, the finding that recreational athletes benefit more from PYR while competitive athletes benefit more from POL suggests that TID should be athlete-level appropriate, not just phase-appropriate.
- **Galán-Rioja et al. (2023)**: Found that traditional periodization in cyclists still commonly follows the pattern of increasing intensity and specificity as competition approaches, consistent with the phase-specific model above.

## 6. Practical Implications for Training Plan Design

### General Rules for Training Plan Generation

1. **Z1 is the foundation, always**: Regardless of athlete level or phase, 70–85% of total training time should be low intensity (below first ventilatory/lactate threshold)

2. **Athlete level matters more than a single "model"**:
   - **Recreational athletes (<7 h/week)**: SAFER to use pyramidal distribution; moderate work provides needed training stimulus density. A polarized approach works but may under-stimulate.
   - **Competitive athletes (>10 h/week)**: STRONG EVIDENCE for polarized distribution; avoid the "grey zone" of moderate intensity
   - **Elite athletes (>15 h/week)**: Polarized with very little Z2 during base; more pyramidal during competition phase

3. **TID varies by training phase**:
   - **Base**: Maximally polarized (fewest Z2 sessions)
   - **Build**: Pyramidal shift — add race-specific intensity
   - **Peak**: Reduce volume, maintain intensity, but more Z1 proportionally (volume drops faster than Z3 time)
   - **Taper**: Z1-dominant with very brief, high-quality Z3 "priming" sessions

4. **Key decision points for plan generation**:
   - Athlete's weekly training hours → determines feasibility of polarized vs pyramidal
   - Training phase → determines composition of the ~20% non-Z1 time
   - Event duration → determines specificity of Z2 vs Z3 emphasis
   - Athlete's training history → training age and accumulated base influence tolerance and responsiveness

5. **Caution — avoid "threshold trap"**: Training at Z2 almost exclusively (the most common mistake among self-coached athletes) provides the worst stimulus-to-fatigue ratio. Even under a pyramidal model, Z2 should rarely exceed 15–20% of total time.
